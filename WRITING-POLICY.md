# Writing Policy & Voice Guide

**Jennifer Perret — jenp.ai**

_Living document. Update it whenever a new piece reveals a pattern, or the voice shifts._

This is one document doing two jobs. The **policy** is the standard: why I write and what a piece has to clear before it goes out. The **voice guide** is the craft: how it should sound when it does. Neither works without the other — a piece can be principled and lifeless, or charming and hollow. I want both.

---

## Purpose

Writing is how I think, not just how I publish. It's where I work out what I actually believe, and where I earn someone's attention rather than assume it. That holds for blog posts, customer conversations, and anything else with my name on it.

I'll lean on AI for outlines, framing, research, and for catching what I missed. I won't hand over the thinking. The point isn't to fill space faster — it's to make each piece something I'd be proud to have my name on.

---

## The Principles

### 1. The writing should be unmistakably mine

> "I write entirely to find out what I'm thinking, what I'm looking at, what I see, and what it means. What I want and what I fear." — Joan Didion

Strip my name off a post and someone should still know it's me. Voice is a muscle; skip the reps and it atrophies. AI can draft, research, restructure, and argue with me — but the judgment, the stumbles, and the through-line have to be mine.

### 2. Hard writing is the whole point

> "The time to begin writing an article is when you have finished it to your satisfaction." — Mark Twain

The agony *is* the work. Writing, re-writing, adjusting until it's right is where the thinking actually happens. A piece that was easy to produce is usually a piece I hadn't thought hard enough about. If I can't defend every claim in it under questioning, it isn't done.

### 3. Respect the reader's time

> "Use the time of a total stranger in such a way that they will not feel the time was wasted." — Kurt Vonnegut

If a reader spends more time reading it than I spent making it, I got the ratio backwards. There should be no sentence I can't stand behind. "Oh, that part was just the AI, ignore it" means I didn't do my job.

### 4. Be clear, be concise, be witty

> "Eschew surplusage." — Mark Twain
>
> "The difference between the almost right word and the right word is really a large matter — 'tis the difference between the lightning-bug and the lightning." — Mark Twain

LLMs favor length over clarity, and sprawl is a good place to hide a contradiction. Cut hard. But cutting isn't the same as flattening — aim for Twain's register, not Shakespeare's: dry, self-deprecating, a little mischievous, landing the point with a grin instead of a flourish. Wit earns attention that word count never will.

_(Worth noting: "brevity is the soul of wit" is Polonius — Shakespeare's windbag — which tells you something about taking brevity advice from long-winded sources.)_

### 5. Name the failure before the lesson

My credibility comes from saying plainly what went wrong — the ungoverned agents, the wasted weekend, the branch named `please-work` — not from tidy narratives where I was right all along. Confession first, insight second.

### 6. Use AI to buy thinking time, not to skip it

The real question isn't "can I make it go faster?" It's what to delegate so I have *more* room to think, pause, write, and re-write until it's right. AI drafts and gathers. I decide what's true and what it means.

## The Voice

### Tone & rhythm

Open with a raw, personal beat before any analysis — "I feel this deeply," not "In this post I'll cover." Mix short, punchy sentences with longer reflective ones; that variation is the heartbeat of the thing. Use repetition deliberately when it earns emphasis ("writing, re-writing, adjusting"). State struggle plainly, without hedging: "the hardest and least successful year in my career."

### The arc most pieces follow

1. **Hook** — a reaction to someone else's insight or an external prompt.
2. **Turn** — the personal story or struggle that proves it.
3. **Reframe** — what it taught me.
4. **Bridge to now** — connect the old lesson to a present tension, often speed versus depth.
5. **Close** — a question or provocation. Not a tidy bow.

### Voice traits

First person, confessional, leadership through vulnerability. Dry, Twain-flavored wit — self-deprecating and a little mischievous, never arch or performatively clever. Values-driven contrasts do a lot of work ("drive to results" versus "lock myself down and focus"). "We" and "us" to universalize a lesson, "I" whenever the moment is genuinely personal. Failure named directly, without corporate softening.

### Signature moves

"The real question is…" · "It taught me so much." · "…until you get it right." · Gratitude called out by name.

### What to avoid

Heavy sub-headers and listicle scaffolding — they read as documentation, not as a letter. Long explanatory throat-clearing before anything personal arrives. Third-person distancing ("we built," "our team") where "I" carries more weight. Tidy, resolved endings; replace them with an open question. And anything that exists mainly to fill space.

## Before I publish

**Craft**

- [ ] Does it open with a felt reaction, not a topic statement?
- [ ] Is there a genuine stumble named plainly?
- [ ] Do sentence lengths vary — short and punchy next to longer and reflective?
- [ ] Is repetition used on purpose, where it earns emphasis?
- [ ] Does it close with a question or provocation instead of a summary?
- [ ] Have I cut headers and lists where prose would carry more weight?
- [ ] Is "we" doing work that "I" should be doing?

**Standard**

- [ ] Would someone recognize this as mine with the byline removed?
- [ ] Is there a sentence here I couldn't defend in a live Q&A?
- [ ] Did I cut every word that was doing no work?
- [ ] Is there at least one moment of genuine wit, not just competence?
- [ ] Did the writing change my own mind about anything? If not, did I actually think?

---

## How I work with AI on a piece

1. AI helps with the outline, the framing, the research, and the argument I'm missing.
2. I write the draft — or rewrite AI's — until the thinking is mine.
3. We iterate. That back-and-forth *is* the work; it mirrors the lesson that started all of this.
4. Every five to ten posts, I come back and revise this document as the voice evolves.
